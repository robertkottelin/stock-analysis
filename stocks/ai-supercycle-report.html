1 · First principles — what the supercycle actually is

A supercycle is not “AI is cool.” It is a multi-year period in which incremental economic value from a general-purpose technology exceeds the cost of deploying it at scale, so capital, talent, and equity risk premia reorganise around that gap. Three nested claims are often conflated; only the first is nearly certain.

Claim A — Capability (true)

  • Foundation models are a new production function for prediction, generation, and software labour.
  • Training and inference require orders of magnitude more FLOPs and memory bandwidth than classical enterprise IT.
  • That creates a real, multi-year physical buildout: chips → packaging → servers → power → networks → software.

Claim B — Profit (contested)

  • Someone must convert FLOPs into cash returns above WACC.
  • Today most accounting profit sits in the toolmakers (accelerators, HBM, networking, power).
  • Application-layer profit is large in theory, thin in reported numbers for most pure-play AI software.

Claim C — Equity prices (most fragile)

  • Markets price the present value of expected free cash flows, not FLOPs.
  • Prices can rise while economics are still “capex first, ROI later” — until the market demands proof.
  • The supercycle for prices ends (or pauses) when either (1) incremental spend stops growing, (2) margins mean-revert on supply, or (3) discount rates / risk appetite reprice duration.
Working definition used in this report. The AI supercycle is the period during which global AI-related capital expenditure and the market capitalisation of AI-exposed equities are both dominated by infrastructure scaling rather than by mature application cash flows. By that definition we are still inside the supercycle — but the easiest price gains from “discover the theme” are behind us. What remains is more selective, more cyclical, and more dependent on ROI proof.

Why this is not “just another tech cycle”

Three first-principles differences from a normal SaaS or smartphone cycle:

  1. Energy and atoms bind before software does. You can ship code overnight; you cannot site a multi-GW data centre overnight. Power interconnect queues, transformers, water, and advanced packaging are rate-limiters with 2–5 year lead times.
  2. Unit economics are inverted early. Classic software: low marginal cost, high gross margin from day one. Frontier AI: enormous fixed cost of training clusters, still-evolving inference cost curves, and revenue that often lags capability.
  3. The customer set is oligopolistic. A handful of hyperscalers and frontier labs set the demand curve for GPUs, HBM, and networking. Their capex guidance is the cycle’s near-term GDP of AI infrastructure.

AWS, Azure, GCP, Oracle. Monetise via tokens, reserved capacity, and enterprise contracts. Capex-heavy FCF trough years.
Investing phase

Long-duration option
Implication. As long as packaging, HBM, and power remain scarce, economic rents stay upstream. When those bottlenecks clear, unit prices fall, volumes may still rise, and equity multiples on pure infrastructure names compress even if AI “adoption” keeps growing. That is the classic commodity-cycle trap inside a technology supercycle.

Order-of-magnitude spend (contextual, not a model)

Public hyperscaler AI-related capital budgets have scaled into the hundreds of billions of dollars per year collectively (Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, plus Oracle and others). Single-name plans on the order of ~$100–200bn annual capex for the largest platforms have been disclosed or guided in 2025–26 commentary. That is the demand pulse for GPUs, HBM, networking gear, generators, and interconnect hardware.

For equity prices, the relevant derivative is not the level of spend — it is the second derivative: acceleration, stable high plateau, or deceleration. Markets re-rate on acceleration; they de-rate on deceleration even if the absolute level remains historically enormous.

3 · Four profit phases — who earns when

Think of the AI economy as a staged industrial buildout. Sectors do not “all win for ten years.” They take turns at the top of the P&L waterfall.

Phase 1 · 2023–2027ish
Picks & shovels
Accelerators, HBM/memory, advanced foundry/packaging, cluster networking, power equipment. Scarcity + hyperscaler opex-as-capex drives exceptional margins and multiple expansion.
Winners: NVDA ecosystem, MU/HBM chain, TSMC packaging, ANET/optics, VRT/ETN/power
Phase 2 · 2026–2030
Platforms & power
Cloud platforms convert capacity into utilisation and contract revenue. Utilities, independent power, and grid OEMs monetise multi-year load growth. Custom ASICs dilute pure-GPU share of FLOPs.
Winners: MSFT/AMZN/GOOGL cloud, ORCL capacity, CEG-type power, select industrials
Phase 3 · 2028–2035
Application cash flows
Vertical software, agents inside workflows, advertising/search re-architecture, industrial automation, healthcare imaging, defence autonomy. Profit concentrates where distribution + proprietary data exist.
Winners: incumbents with data/distribution; few pure-play model cos
Phase 4 · 2030s
Commoditised intelligence
Inference cost collapses; models become a utility input. Economic surplus shifts to labour displacement capture, product redesign, and capital-light orchestration layers — or is competed away to consumers.
Winners: unclear; many Phase-1 multiples already dead

4.5 What to look for — Phase 3 checklist

A company fits Phase 3 when most of these are true:

Must-haves

  1. System of record or distribution — CRM, ERP, EHR, IDE, OS, marketplace, daily habit surface
  2. Proprietary data that improves with use — workflow logs, transactions, clinical outcomes, fleet telemetry
  3. Willingness-to-pay proof — paid AI SKU; gross margin after inference cost
  4. Retention / NRR — AI attach raises NRR; no churn when a cheaper model appears
  5. Switching costs in the workflow — agents write into tickets, repos, ledgers — not a chat tab
  6. Unit economics positive at scale — cost per action ≪ price per action
  7. Defensibility vs foundation models — clear answer to “why won’t MSFT/GOOGL bundle this?”
  8. Regulatory / trust moat (optional) — healthcare, finance, defence, legal

Red flags (Phase 3 impostors)

  • AI revenue not broken out, or “AI-powered” with no pricing power
  • Gross margin down structurally because they resell tokens
  • Customers are other AI startups (circular demand)
  • Multiple assumes Phase 3 cash flows while metrics are still Phase 1 hype
  • No path if open-source matches closed models on quality

4.6 What to look for — Phase 4 checklist

Phase 4 winners often do not brand as AI. Screen for:

Must-haves

  1. Outcome pricing, not model pricing — miles, claims, ads converted, uptime
  2. Labour cost large and replaceable — support, coding factories, document ops
  3. Physical or regulatory lock-in — devices, hospitals, airframes, factory lines
  4. Balance sheet to buy disrupted assets — bury AI wrappers in the product
  5. Orchestration / identity / compliance — control agent permissions when models swap
  6. Consumer surplus via ads or take-rate — if intelligence is free, attention still pays
  7. Multiples that don’t require scarcity — often look like boring compounders (15–25× E)

Screening questions

  • If inference cost → 0, does pricing power rise or collapse?
  • If open-source matches closed models, what is left?
  • Is the customer buying intelligence or a job done?
  • Who pays when the model is wrong — is liability a moat?
  • Would this company still win if “AI” vanished from every slide deck?

5 · How much is still left on the table

Separate three “tables”: physical capacity still to build, earnings still to be reported, and equity upside still available after what is already priced.

5.1 Physical table — large

On first principles, global useful AI compute is still a small fraction of the steady-state required if models become a default layer in software, customer service, coding, science, and robotics. Power alone caps how fast the world can add GPU-equivalent capacity. Multi-year backlogs in transformers, turbines, and grid interconnect imply that the buildout of atoms continues into the early 2030s even if model architecture improves efficiency 2–5×.

Efficiency gains do not end the cycle; they change the shape. Better models per FLOP can increase demand (Jevons) if price elasticity of inference is high — the historical pattern for compute. They can also crash near-term GPU ASPs if customers pause while waiting for the next architecture. Both can be true in sequence.

5.2 Earnings table — medium, skewed upstream

Near-term reported profit growth remains concentrated in semiconductors and select infrastructure. Hyperscalers are spending at a rate that deliberately suppresses free cash flow (see Amazon’s AI-capex wave in this toolkit’s Amazon pipeline). That means:

  • Upstream earnings: still substantial runway if units grow and pricing holds — until supply catches demand.
  • Midstream cloud earnings: delayed; the “table” is utilisation and pricing power 2027–30, not 2026 EPS.
  • Downstream app earnings: mostly not yet on the table as cash; they are duration assets.

5.3 Equity table — smaller than the physical table

This is the crucial distinction. Much of the easy re-rating from “AI is real” to “AI is the budget” has already occurred in mega-cap tech and the semiconductor complex. What remains is not zero — but it is path-dependent:

Still on the table

  • Power & grid if load forecasts keep rising and regulated returns stay intact.
  • Memory/packaging through the next shortage leg — with violent cyclicality.
  • Networking/optics as cluster scale and 800G/1.6T ramps continue.
  • Cloud FCF recovery if utilisation catches capex (2027–29 window).
  • Select application winners with distribution (not model cos by default).
  • Industrial content (robotics, factories, connectors, automation) as a slower second wave.

Mostly already claimed

  • “AI existence” premium in Mag7 multiples — partially, not fully, mean-reverted.
  • First-wave narrative beta in many small AI software names (lots of terminal value, little cash).
  • One-decision GPU scarcity as a pure story — now contested by custom silicon and efficiency.
  • Zero-rate duration free lunch — higher real rates already tax long-duration AI claims.
Blunt answer to “how much is left?” On physical buildout: years and trillions of cumulative capex. On equity alpha from simply owning “AI”: materially less than 2023–24. The remaining table is in relative performance — Phase-2 power/platforms vs Phase-1 tools, quality compounders vs narrative shells, and cash-flow proof vs slide-deck total addressable market.

6 · How long will AI still drive prices up?

Split the question into (a) how long AI drives earnings, (b) how long it drives multiples, and (c) how long it dominates the equity factor that moves indices.

2023–2025 · completed
Discovery + multiple expansion
ChatGPT moment → hyperscaler capex step-function → semiconductor and Mag7 re-rating. Prices led earnings; narrative was enough.
2026–2027 · now
Earnings delivery + scrutiny
Capex still rising or plateauing at extreme levels. Market rewards beat-and-raise in tools and proof of utilisation in cloud. First serious “ROI audit” window. Index leadership still AI-heavy but narrower.
2027–2029 · transition
Second derivative decides prices
If capex growth slows while utilisation ramps, cloud and software can lead even as GPU growth decelerates. If capex keeps accelerating without ROI, a valuation air-pocket hits duration tech. Power and industrials can keep compounding on backlog.
2030–2035 · maturity
AI as default infrastructure
AI stops being a separate “trade” and becomes a feature of almost every quality growth equity. Index-level “AI beta” fades; stock-picking inside winners/losers dominates. Physical spend continues but at lower growth rates.

Direct answer

As a broad market driver of upside (higher prices across a wide AI basket): likely another 1–3 years of episodic leadership, not a straight line — with increasing drawdowns and factor rotation. The period of “almost anything AI-related goes up” is already over.

As a driver of earnings in specific layers: 5–10+ years for power, advanced semiconductors, and cloud infrastructure; longer for industrial and software embedding. Earnings can rise for a decade while multiples compress for five of those years.

As a driver of passive index returns: as long as Mag7/hyperscalers dominate cap-weighted indices and their cash flows compound. That can outlast the “AI trade” brand by many years — or fail if regulation, competition, or ROI disappointment hits the oligopoly simultaneously.

7 · Pauses, drawdowns, and the internet-bubble parallel

7.1 Will there be pauses? Yes — almost certainly

First principles: any system with multi-year lead times and oligopolistic buyers produces inventory cycles. Expect pauses driven by:

  • Absorption gaps — clusters ordered, not yet fully utilised; customers digest capacity.
  • Architecture transitions — wait for next GPU/ASIC generation; temporary order freeze.
  • Power delays — chips ready, megawatts not; spend slips right.
  • ROI narrative breaks — one or two high-profile enterprise write-downs or hyperscaler capex cuts.
  • Macro / rates — higher discount rates hit long-duration AI claims regardless of FLOPs.
  • Policy — export controls, energy permitting, model regulation.

Pauses are not the end of the supercycle. In semiconductors, pauses are how the cycle works. The danger is treating every pause as terminal — or treating every rally as permanent.

The correct bubble framing. AI is not “all bubble” or “no bubble.” It is a barbell: (1) a real industrial buildout with cash-flowing oligopolies, and (2) a speculative halo of duration claims, circular financing risks, and TAM slide decks. The internet pattern that will repeat is survivorship: the technology transforms the economy; most equity stories from the peak do not compound from that peak. The pattern that may not fully repeat is a multi-year total wipeout of the infrastructure complex — because today’s buyers are the cash machines of the modern economy, not pure venture-funded portals.

7.3 What a “pause that rhymes with 2000” would look like

A 2000-style event in AI would not require AI to “fail.” It would require:

  1. Capex growth to decelerate sharply (even from a high base).
  2. Evidence that inference demand does not fill training-driven capacity.
  3. A simultaneous multiple compression in long-duration tech (rates or risk-off).
  4. Inventory corrections in semis that turn 40–70% gross margins into a price war for a few quarters.

That package can cut AI-basket equities 30–60% without falsifying the 10-year adoption thesis — just as fibre cuts did not falsify the internet.

8 · Scenarios, kill-criteria, positioning logic

Bull path (next 5y)

  • Inference demand scales with agents/copilots in production.
  • Hyperscaler ROI metrics (cloud growth, ad ROI, internal productivity) stay convincing.
  • Power buildout proceeds; no multi-year hard stop.
  • Phase-1 tools stay scarce enough to defend margins into 2027.
  • Equity outcome: indices grind up with violent rotations; power + quality semis + cloud lead.

Base path (next 5y)

  • Capex plateaus at high levels after 2026–27.
  • One or two inventory air-pockets in semis (−30–50% peak-to-trough in hot names).
  • Application revenue grows but disappoints relative to 2024–25 narratives.
  • Equity outcome: AI still matters for earnings; multiples compress; stock-picking > basket.

Bear path (next 5y)

  • ROI narrative fails at scale (enterprise projects cancelled; model commoditisation races to free).
  • Coordinated hyperscaler capex cuts of 20%+ YoY.
  • Geopolitical shock to advanced packaging / Taiwan supply.
  • Equity outcome: 2000-like destruction in high-duration AI names; quality platforms with real FCF survive and later re-dominate (Amazon-after-dotcom pattern).

Kill-criteria for the “supercycle still drives prices” thesis

Second derivative flips hard. Combined hyperscaler capex guides down mid-teens+% YoY for two consecutive print seasons without a clear utilisation boom.
Toolmaker margins collapse. Leader accelerator gross margins structurally re-rate from scarcity oligopoly toward competitive silicon (not just one soft quarter).
Cloud AI growth decouples from spend. Capex stays high while cloud AI-related growth decelerates for 4+ quarters — ROI failure mode.
Power hard-stop. Major markets impose multi-year connection freezes that cannot be bypassed with on-site generation at scale.

Positioning logic (framework, not a portfolio)

For a 5–10 year capital allocator thinking in phases:

  • Phase 1 residual: own scarcity only where balance sheets and roadmaps defend oligopoly rents; size for inventory drawdowns.
  • Phase 2 core: power/grid, cloud platforms with FCF recovery optionality, networking as cluster scale continues.
  • Phase 3 options: small sleeves in application winners with distribution; avoid paying Phase-3 terminal value for Phase-1 revenue.
  • Through-cycle compounders: industrials with AI content (automation, connectivity, cooling) — lower beta, longer duration of demand.
  • Hedge the narrative: cash-flow quality and valuation discipline beat thematic purity after the discovery phase ends.

9 · Bottom line

◆ Synthesis

The AI supercycle is real as a physical and earnings phenomenon for most of a decade. As a broad equity price rocket, it is already mid-to-late innings: still capable of lifting markets, no longer capable of lifting everything.

How long will it drive prices up? Expect episodic leadership for ~1–3 more years in index terms, with larger drawdowns and narrower leadership; earnings contribution for 5–10+ years in power, advanced semis, and cloud; and permanent embedding thereafter where AI is just “how software works.”

How much is left on the table? Large in cumulative infrastructure build and eventual application surplus; moderate and selective in equity alpha after 2023–25 re-rating. The remaining edge is phase rotation and quality, not “buy AI.”

Will there be pauses like the internet bubble? Yes to pauses (inventory, ROI scares, rates). Partial rhyme with 2000 for speculative duration assets and any overbuilt layer. Imperfect rhyme for cash-rich hyperscalers and oligopolistic toolmakers — unless ROI fails at scale or supply floods simultaneously.

Who makes the money, when? Phase 1 (now): accelerators, HBM, packaging, networking. Phase 2 (emerging): power, cloud utilisation, systems. Phase 3 (later): application cash flows with distribution. Phase 4: intelligence as a utility input — surplus to incumbents and consumers, not to every AI ticker from the peak.
One-sentence investment stance. Treat AI as a decade-long industrial transformation and a 2–4 year residual equity supercycle with air-pockets — own the scarce layers and the eventual cash-flow platforms, demand proof as you move down the stack, and never confuse “AI will change the world” with “this price is still cheap.”
